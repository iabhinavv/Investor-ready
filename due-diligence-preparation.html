The problem

Deals rarely die in the pitch. They die in diligence.

By the time an investor starts diligence they like the company. What they're looking for now is a reason the story doesn't hold: a contract that doesn't say what the deck implies, a key customer about to churn, an ex-contractor who still owns part of the code.

One issue rarely kills a deal on its own. What kills deals is time and doubt: each surprise adds a week, the investor's confidence drops, and either the price comes down or the term sheet never comes.

Red flags that slow early-stage deals

  • Code or designs written by contractors with no IP assignment
  • Revenue concentrated in one or two customers, with short or no contracts
  • Deck metrics that don't match the accounts
  • Unfiled statutory returns or tax registrations
  • Founder vesting that was never put in place
  • Undisclosed disputes, loans or side agreements

Guide

The investor due diligence checklist, and how to get ahead of it.

What early-stage investors check, area by area, and what we prepare for each.

Legal and corporate

Investors confirm the company exists as described, the founders own what they say, and nothing in the constitution or shareholder agreements blocks the deal. We check incorporation, share issues, board approvals and founder vesting, and brief your lawyer on anything that needs fixing.

Financial

They compare the deck, the model and the accounts. Do revenue and burn match? Are there liabilities the model ignores, such as unpaid taxes, deferred salaries or loans from founders? We reconcile all three and make sure the story is the same everywhere.

Commercial

For companies with revenue, investors often call customers. They look at concentration, contract terms, churn and pipeline quality. The KPI pack shows traction the way investors want to see it: monthly revenue, cohort retention and a pipeline with stages and probabilities.

Intellectual property and technology

Who owns the code, the brand and the data? Every founder, employee and contractor who built something needs a signed IP assignment. We list who has signed and who hasn't, so your lawyer can close the gaps.

Team

Employment contracts, contractor agreements, key-person risk and whether the team can deliver the plan the money is for.

The Q&A bank

We write down the questions investors are most likely to ask about your company, including the uncomfortable ones, and work through your answers with you. "Why hasn't a bigger company done this?" "What happens if your biggest customer leaves?" "Why will this round get you to the next one?" Founders who've answered these on paper answer them better in the room.

Disclose, don't hide. Every company has issues. What hurts is an investor finding one you didn't mention. A known problem with a fix plan attached reads as competence; the same problem found by an analyst reads as risk.

The diligence pack goes into your data room. If you're not sure how much work you need, the Investor-Ready Audit runs a lighter version of the same review in 5 days.

FAQ

Questions about due diligence.

How long does investor due diligence take?

At seed it can be a few days to a few weeks; at Series A, often several weeks. Most of that time is spent waiting for documents and answers. Founders who prepare first shorten it, because they can answer requests the same day.

Do you do legal due diligence?

We run the review and identify legal issues, but we're not a law firm. Fixing them, such as drafting IP assignments or filing corrections, is done by your lawyer or one of our licensed partners, and we coordinate it.

What is pre-emptive due diligence?

Running the investor's diligence on yourself before you raise, so you find and fix issues on your own timeline, not in the middle of a deal.

What if you find something serious?

We tell you plainly, explain how an investor is likely to view it, and lay out the options: fix it, disclose it with a plan, or adjust the timing of the raise.
