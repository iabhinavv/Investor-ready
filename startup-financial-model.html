The problem

Investors don't test your forecast. They test whether you understand it.

No early-stage investor believes year-five revenue. What they're really checking is whether you know what drives the business. What does a customer cost to win? How long until they pay that back? What happens to cash if growth comes in at half the plan?

A model built on "grow 15% a month" can't answer any of that. When an analyst changes one assumption and the model breaks, or the numbers don't match the deck, the meeting stops being about your company and starts being about your spreadsheet.

Red flags investors look for in a model

  • Revenue driven by a growth rate, not by customers, price and conversion
  • No balance sheet, so cash and working capital don't add up
  • Hard-coded numbers buried inside formulas
  • Hiring plan and salaries that don't match the use of funds
  • Figures that differ from the deck

Guide

What makes a financial model investor-grade.

Five things we build into every model, and why investors care about each one.

1. It's driven by real inputs

Revenue is built from the things you control and can evidence: leads, conversion rate, price, churn, expansion. For a SaaS company that might be pipeline × win rate × contract value; for a marketplace, buyers × order frequency × basket × take rate. When an investor asks "what if conversion is 2% not 3%?", you change one cell and every statement updates.

2. The three statements are linked

The profit and loss, balance sheet and cash flow statement are connected so the accounting balances. That matters because startups run out of cash, not profit: receivables, inventory and payment terms can make a "profitable" month a cash-negative one. A model without a balance sheet hides this.

3. Unit economics are visible

Customer acquisition cost (CAC), lifetime value (LTV), payback period and gross margin sit on one page. Investors use them to judge whether more money will make the business bigger and better, or just bigger. Many investors look for LTV of at least 3 times CAC and a payback inside 12–18 months, though the right target depends on your model.

4. Runway and use of funds tie to the round

The model shows how much you're raising, what it's spent on month by month, and how long it lasts. Most seed rounds are sized for 18–24 months of runway to reach the milestones that justify the next round. The model shows which milestones those are and when you hit them.

5. Scenarios are one switch away

Base, upside and downside cases change the key drivers together, so you can show an investor what happens if things go slower than planned, and that you still have a plan.

FAQ

Questions about the financial model.

Why not use a free startup financial model template?

Templates are a good start if you have time to learn them. The hard part isn't the spreadsheet; it's choosing the drivers that fit your business, sourcing every assumption and being able to defend it. That's what you're paying for.

We're pre-revenue. Can you still build a model?

Yes. Pre-revenue models are driven by the assumptions you'll test with the money: pilot conversion, pricing, sales cycle and hiring. We source each one from comparable companies or your own early data, and label which is which.

Excel or Google Sheets?

Either. You get a fully editable file with no locked cells and no add-ins.
